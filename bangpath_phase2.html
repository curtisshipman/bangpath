PHASE 2 BRIEF

Phase 1 got you functional. Open chords clean at 80 BPM, strumming hand trained, A-shape barre working, F-shape developing. Congratulations — you are now precisely as dangerous as a student driver who can start a car.

Phase 2 is where you stop playing chords and start playing guitar. That means: full voicings beyond basic open shapes, chord inversions, a strumming pattern library that covers every rhythmic feel in the setlist, left-hand embellishment that doesn't wreck the beat, power chord fluency for the metal treatment, drop tuning for This Corrosion, and enough song-level endurance to survive a full run-through even if it's ugly.

Everything is mapped to the setlist. No abstract exercises for their own sake. If a technique doesn't show up on stage, it doesn't show up in this program.

⚠ Gear Context — Read Once, Apply Always
All exercises are designed for acoustic guitar as primary instrument. Finger strength requirements are higher. Barre chord tension is greater. Anything that works acoustically transfers clean to electric at the gig. Do not use effects, distortion, or electric-specific techniques during practice. Phase 4 addresses the electric transition. Until then: acoustic only.
PHASE 2 EXIT CRITERIA

These are not suggestions. You do not advance to Phase 3 until every item is checked. Self-assessment is allowed but be honest with yourself — perform each check against a metronome and record it.

  • Full voicing versions of at least 8 setlist songs playable at reduced tempo (≥ 75% of original) with consistent rhythm
  • Strumming patterns for waltz feel, driving eighth notes, and syncopated stabs all accessible on demand — can switch between them mid-practice without setup time
  • Left-hand embellishment (hammer-ons and pull-offs within a chord shape) executable without losing the beat
  • Barre chord transitions: F to C, E to A, G to D with barre — smooth enough that a listener doesn't hear the gap
  • Can play through a full song form start to finish, even imperfectly — no stopping, no restarting, no "let me take that again"

FULL VOICING VOCABULARY
WEEKS 1–3

Open chords got you through Phase 1. Now you build the vocabulary that makes you sound like a guitar player with options rather than a beginner who memorized five shapes. Every voicing here maps to at least one setlist song.

3.1 — The CAGED System: Why It Matters Right Now

CAGED means any chord can be played in five different positions on the neck. You do not need to master all five positions for every chord in Phase 2. You need to understand the concept and apply it to two or three positions per chord. This is what allows you to play In a Big Country in D without camping at the second fret the entire song.

Concept: CAGED Shapes
Five chord shapes — C, A, G, E, D — each root to a different string. Move any shape up the neck using the barre to change key. The shape stays the same. The fret changes. Master this and you can play any chord in any position.

✓ What Good Voicing Execution Looks and Feels Like
You press down on a chord and strum all strings — and every string that should ring is ringing. No buzzing. No muted strings you didn't intend to mute. The chord sounds full and clean on the first press, not after you "settle in." You can arrive at the chord cleanly in motion, not just from a standing start. That's the bar.

3.3 — Inversions: Why They Matter for !bang!path!

An inversion just means a chord note other than the root is in the bass. For your role as rhythm guitarist in a band context, the bass player handles the fundamental root. That means you can use inversions to create melodic movement in your chord progression without the arrangement sounding muddy or redundant.

The two inversions above (Bb/D and G/B) are your starting toolkit. They create passing motion — the bass note walks stepwise between chord roots. Used in Broken Wings and the Smiths track, they make the harmonic movement feel inevitable rather than chunky.

// Feel Guide: Inversions
A correctly voiced inversion feels like the chord is leaning toward the next one. It has direction. A root position chord just sits. When you play Bb/D moving to Eb, you should feel the bass note pulling you forward. If it feels static, check that your D string is actually voiced as the D note.

05 //
LEFT HAND EMBELLISHMENT
WEEKS 5–6 — HAMMER-ONS AND PULL-OFFS WITHIN CHORD SHAPES

Embellishment is what separates a rhythm guitarist who plays chords from one who plays arrangements. The goal in Phase 2 is narrow: hammer-ons and pull-offs within an existing chord shape, executed without losing the beat. You are not doing lead licks. You are adding texture from inside the rhythm.

⚡ Critical Rule
The beat comes first. Every time. If adding the embellishment disrupts the strumming pattern rhythm, you have failed the embellishment. The embellishment is decoration. The rhythm is the structure. Decoration does not compromise structure.
TECH 01 //
HAMMER-ON FROM OPEN (Sus Decoration)
▸ In a Big Country · Milky Way

While holding a G or D chord, hammer your pinky (4th finger) onto the 3rd or 4th fret of the high strings to create a sus2 or sus4 color, then release back to the chord tone.

e |–3–h5–3–|–3–h5–3–| (G chord, pinky hammers to 5th fret) B |–3–––––3–|–3–––––3–| (hold B string) G |–0–––––0–|–0–––––0–| (open, don't touch) D |–0–––––0–|–0–––––0–| (open) A |–x–––––x–|–x–––––x–| E |–3–––––3–|–3–––––3–| h = hammer-on. Strike the 5th fret without picking.

Practice steps: (1) Hold the G chord shape cleanly. (2) Without lifting other fingers, place pinky on e string 5th fret — don't pick it, hammer it. (3) Lift pinky. (4) Integrate into the strum: strum, hammer mid-strum, continue strumming.

// What Good Execution Sounds Like
The hammer note rings clearly without a pick stroke. You can hear a distinct pitch on the hammered note — not a buzz, not a thud. The chord underneath does not change. Your strumming hand doesn't stutter when the left hand embellishes.
TECH 02 //
PULL-OFF DECORATION (Am Shape)
▸ Everyday Is Like Sunday · Tainted Love

From an Am chord, pull off the second fret of the B string back to open. This creates the Amsus4 → Am move that Morrissey-era Marr arrangements use constantly. It's a one-finger embellishment that doesn't move the whole chord.

e |–0–| B |–1–p0–| (1st fret held, 2nd fret pulled to open) G |–2–| D |–2–| A |–0–| E |–x–| p = pull-off. Flick the fretting finger downward to sound the open string.

The pulling finger flicks slightly downward as it releases — it doesn't just lift. The downward motion catches the string and makes it ring without a pick. Practice this slowly until the pull-off rings as loud as a picked note.

// What Good Execution Sounds Like
Both notes — the fretted and the open — ring at similar volume. The transition is smooth, not two separate plucks. The chord underneath is undisturbed. This sounds like the chord is sighing or breathing, not like someone is poking the strings.
TECH 03 //
SUSPENDED RESOLUTION (Dsus4 → D)
▸ In a Big Country · Suburbia · Whole of the Moon

The classic D sus-to-resolved move. Lift your third finger off the B string (or hammer back onto it) to toggle between Dsus4 and D. This is a foundational embellishment that appears in almost every song in a D-based arrangement.

Dsus4 D Dsus4 D e |–3–| e |–2–| e |–3–| e |–2–| B |–3–| → B |–3–| → B |–3–| → B |–3–| G |–2–| G |–2–| G |–2–| G |–2–| D |–0–| D |–0–| D |–0–| D |–0–| Only the e string changes: 3rd fret → 2nd fret. One finger toggles the suspension.
// What Good Execution Sounds Like
The sus4 creates mild harmonic tension. The resolution to D releases it. This happens within the strumming pattern — you don't stop strumming to make the finger adjustment. It's a micro-movement inside a larger rhythmic motion. If you're stopping to adjust, slow down the tempo until you can do it in motion.
06 //
POWER CHORDS — FLUENCY TRAINING
WEEK 7 — THE METAL TREATMENT FOUNDATION

Power chords are two-note (root + fifth) or three-note (root + fifth + octave) shapes with no third. They sound heavy in any context because the interval of a fifth is acoustically neutral — neither major nor minor. This is exactly what the metal treatment of new wave songs needs: the harmonic ambiguity of a fifth sitting under a melody that originally came from a synthesizer.

Acoustic Power Chord Reality Check
Power chords on acoustic sound different from electric with distortion — they won't have the same saturation. That's fine. You're practicing clean execution of the shape and movement. The distortion arrives in Phase 4. For now: clean power chords on acoustic build the muscle memory and positioning that the electric stage will rely on.

07 //
DROP D TUNING
WEEK 7 — THIS CORROSION SPECIFICALLY

Drop D means: tune the low E string down one whole step to D. Result: the lowest three strings become D–A–D, which means you can play D5 power chords with a one-finger barre across strings 4, 5, 6. This enables the Corrosion-style riff movement that doesn't work any other way.

⚠ Important
Drop D is a dedicated setup. This Corrosion is the only setlist song in Drop D. You'll want to tune and practice it separately, not mid-session. When performing live, this either means a guitar change or a tuning break. Plan for this in Phase 3 when you address live logistics. For now: practice it in its own dedicated block.

7.1 — How to Tune to Drop D

  1. Tune all strings to standard E A D G B e first.
  2. Play the D string (4th string) — this is your reference pitch.
  3. Turn the tuning peg for the low E string to lower its pitch.
  4. Stop when the low E string sounds one octave below the D string — same pitch class, lower octave.
  5. Verify: play the 7th fret of the low string — it should match the open A string exactly.

FLOOR WEEK PROTOCOL
FOR 2-SESSION WEEKS

Some weeks are 2 sessions. That's reality. Here's how to make 2 sessions count without losing ground:

Floor Week Rules
  • Session 1 (Wednesday): Drill the single hardest thing from your last full week. 45 minutes, one technique or one song, concentrated focus. Do not try to cover everything.
  • Session 2 (Saturday/weekend): Play songs. Your three most playable songs, start to finish, no stopping. This keeps the song-level muscle memory alive even when technique drilling is reduced.
  • Accept the pace hit: A 2-session week is worth about 1 full week of a 5-session week in terms of skill consolidation. If you have 3 or more floor weeks in a row, extend Phase 2 by 2 additional weeks rather than racing the 10-week target.

MASTER FEEL GUIDE
WHAT GOOD EXECUTION SOUNDS AND FEELS LIKE — CONSOLIDATED
// Full Voicings
Every string rings. No buzz. No unintentional mutes. The chord sounds full even on the first press, not after you "settle in." You can arrive there cleanly while moving, not just from a static start.
// Driving Eighths
The hand moves like a pendulum. Consistent weight on every strum. You can sustain it for 4 minutes without tempo drift. The accent beats feel slightly heavier, not louder — they have more attack, not more volume.
// Syncopated Stabs
The stab notes are short and percussive. They pop and die. The contrast between sustained notes and stabs is audible. The groove lives in the contrast, not in either element alone. If everything sounds the same length, the muting isn't working.
// Waltz Feel
You are slightly swaying. Beat 1 has gravity. Beats 2–3 are the lift. The pattern has a rocking motion that feels like it carries you to the next bar. If you have to consciously count to stay in 3/4, you haven't felt it yet — keep going until the count disappears into the body.
// Embellishment Within Chords
The embellishment note rings clearly without a pick stroke. The chord underneath does not change. The strumming hand does not stutter when the left hand embellishes. A listener should hear decoration, not interruption.
// Power Chords
Clean, full, no buzz. Only the intended strings ring. The transition between chords has no gap — you arrive and strike on the beat. The attack is immediate and percussive. On acoustic without distortion, power chords should sound tight and solid, not thin.
// Barre Chord Transitions
A listener does not hear the gap between chords. Your left hand arrives at the new shape before the beat hits. The transition feels like sliding, not jumping. On acoustic, the pressure required is significant — but it should feel like consistent firm pressure, not desperate clenching. Clenching causes fatigue and slows transitions.
// Playing Through a Full Song
You do not stop. You do not restart. You do not apologize to the metronome. A bad chord that kept the beat is always better than a stop. Songs played imperfectly from start to finish build more performance skill than perfect run-throughs of individual sections. The discipline of continuing is the exercise.
