// The Deceptive Song

Shout looks easy on paper — four chords, whole song, 98 BPM. It's not easy. The challenge is the syncopated strumming pattern, which the chord chart documents explicitly. The original has a very specific rhythmic feel — restless, pushing, never quite settling on the beat — that comes entirely from how the pattern lands. Four chords means there's nowhere to hide behind chord complexity. The pattern has to be right or the song feels wrong.

Additionally: this song starts on the chorus. No intro, no verse first — the very first thing you play is Gm into the hook. That's unusual and needs to be drilled specifically so the opening doesn't catch you off guard live.

// All Barre — No Open Chords

Unlike In a Big Country where you got two free open chords, Shout has zero open chord shapes. Every chord in this song is a barre. Gm and Ebmaj7 are fret-3 barre chords. Csus2 is fret-3. Bb6 is a partial barre. Your index finger does not get a rest in this song. Make sure barre fatigue doesn't set in during the long chorus repeats.

// Ebmaj7 — The Unusual Chord

Ebmaj7 is not a common rock chord — it has a distinctly jazzy, open quality that gives Shout its specific emotional colour. In the metal arrangement don't try to replace it with a power chord — the Ebmaj7 is what makes the chorus feel sophisticated rather than just heavy. The partial barre shape (muting the A and e strings) takes some practice to execute cleanly. The muting needs to be deliberate, not accidental.

THE PATTERN
THIS IS THE ENTIRE TECHNICAL CHALLENGE

The chord chart gives the strumming pattern explicitly for the whole song. It is not a simple pattern. It's syncopated, with upstrokes and downstrokes alternating in a way that pushes against the beat. This is the thing to master — not the chords.

The skip on beat 1 "and" is the key. Your arm moves downward on that subdivision but the pick misses the strings. Keeping the arm moving on the skip is what makes the pattern feel fluid rather than choppy.
// The Most Common Mistake

Stopping your arm on the skip at beat 1 "and." When you stop the arm to avoid hitting the strings, the pattern immediately stiffens and the groove dies. The arm must keep moving continuously through all 8 subdivisions — the skip is achieved by lifting the pick slightly away from the strings mid-downstroke, not by stopping the arm. This feels counterintuitive and takes deliberate practice to automate.

Chords Gm – Ebmaj7 – Csus2 – Gm · same chorus sequence × 2 passes
Your job during the solo: Identical to the chorus — same chords, same pattern. The solo is the lead guitarist's moment; your job is to not be interesting. Lock the pattern, hold the tempo, give the lead a solid rhythmic platform to play over. Do not vary the pattern, do not add dynamics, do not embellish. Be a metronome with chords.
05 //
TECHNIQUE NOTES
THREE THINGS TO NAIL
TECH 01 //
THE SKIP — ARM NEVER STOPS

The fundamental problem: On beat 1 "and," the pattern requires a downward arm motion that does not hit the strings. Most guitarists instinctively stop the arm to avoid the strings. This kills the groove immediately.

The correct motion: The pick arm moves in a continuous pendulum. The skip is achieved by rotating the wrist slightly so the pick angle lifts away from the string surface during the downstroke. The arm keeps moving. The strings are missed because the pick is angled away, not because the arm stopped.

Drill: Start with open strings, no chord. 60 BPM. Play the pattern continuously for 2 minutes. Focus exclusively on keeping the arm moving through the skip. Only add a chord once the skip feels natural with open strings.

TECH 02 //
EBMAJ7 STRING MUTING

The challenge: Ebmaj7 requires muting the low E and the high e strings while keeping B, G, and D ringing. In the middle of the syncopated pattern at 98 BPM, a misfired strum hitting those muted strings produces a harsh, buzzing note that cuts through the mix.

The fix: The fretting hand does the work — index finger lightly touching (not pressing) the muted strings. The pick arm's range of motion should be calibrated to hit roughly strings 2-4 (B, G, D) without grazing the low E. Practice Gm → Ebmaj7 transitions specifically: the chord change and the muting adjustment happen simultaneously.

TECH 03 //
CHORUS-OPENS-SONG DRILL

The specific challenge: Walking out on stage, picking up the guitar, and playing the correct chord with the correct pattern with zero warmup bars. This is more psychologically difficult than technically difficult — there is no "easing into" the song.

The drill: Practice the song opening in isolation. Put the guitar down. Wait 10 seconds. Pick it up. Pre-fret the Gm barre chord. On your internal count of 4, launch the pattern on beat 1. Do this 20 times in a row. The cold-start Gm needs to become as automatic as your name.

In band context: Establish a clear cue with your band for when the song starts — a count-in, a drum hit, a nod from whoever is counting. Do not leave the start of this song ambiguous in rehearsal.

// What Good Execution Sounds Like
The pattern has an unmistakable forward push — like the song is always leaning slightly into the next beat. The skip on beat 1 "and" creates a brief lift that gives the pattern its tension. The Ebmaj7 chord has a slightly open, sophisticated quality that keeps the song from feeling too blunt. The verse (Bb6 · Csus2) feels darker and more confrontational than the chorus. The chorus feels like release. The solo section is solid and invisible — you are the floor, not the ceiling. The outro builds intensity through repetition alone — same pattern, same chords, played with increasing conviction until the band cuts it on a final Gm. The crowd knows when it's coming. Hit it hard.

// Build the Pattern Before the Tempo

Unlike other songs where you start slow and build up, with Shout you need to get the syncopated pattern mechanically correct at a slow tempo before increasing anything. A wrong pattern at 98 BPM is just a wrong pattern faster. Start at 60 BPM and make the skip feel natural before you touch 70.
