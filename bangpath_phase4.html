ACOUSTIC TO ELECTRIC TRANSITION PROTOCOL

Three phases on acoustic was the right call. Your fretting hand is strong, your barre chords work under real resistance, and your pick attack is calibrated for a guitar that requires genuine effort to make noise. All of that transfers. Here's what doesn't, and what to do about it.

WHAT ACTUALLY CHANGES

Parameter Acoustic (What You Know) Electric (What's Coming) Adjustment Required
Action High — strings far from fretboard, significant pressure required Low — strings close, minimal pressure required Reduce fretting pressure immediately. Over-gripping causes buzzing, fatigue, and pitch sharpness.
String gauge Typically .012–.053 or heavier, high tension Typically .010–.046, lower tension Bends and vibrato you didn't mean to do will happen. Learn to stay flat intentionally.
Pick attack Loud acoustic requires significant attack to project Amp does the work — attack controls dynamics and tone character Lighten up immediately. You'll overdrive the tone with acoustic-trained attack. Recalibrate in first week.

Noise floor Whatever you don't play = silence Whatever you don't mute = audible hum, buzz, harmonic bleed Your right-hand palm muting and left-hand muting habits need conscious development.
Feedback sensitivity None at practice volume Real at band volume, especially with gain Learn where your guitar feeds back in the room. Control it. It's not an emergency.
Body resonance feel Acoustic body vibrates against you — physical feedback of playing Less physical resonance, more electronic output You may feel "disconnected" at first. This is normal and passes in about a week of regular play.
Neck width / profile Varies — typically wider, deeper Varies by guitar — likely different from your acoustic Assess each electric. The PRS Vela and Revstar both have distinct neck profiles. Pick one for Phase 4 and stay on it.

Single coils at high gain in a band context is a noise management problem you don't need to solve on top of everything else.

If the Kiesel has humbuckers: use the Kiesel. If not, use the Revstar. If for some reason neither feels right in the first two weeks, switch — but then commit again.

ELECTRIC TRANSITION WEEK 1 PROTOCOL

The first week on electric is a deliberate recalibration week. You are not learning new things. You are relearning the same things at a different resistance setting.

DAY 1 — First Contact (30 minutes)

Plug in. Clean tone, no effects, amp at conversation volume.
Play every open chord you know. Observe: how little pressure do you actually need?
Play every barre chord shape in your vocabulary. Do not squeeze harder than necessary for clean tone.
Run through "Tainted Love" chord progression slowly. Notice what's different.
Do NOT turn on any gain or distortion today. Clean only. You are assessing, not performing.
DAY 2 — Pick Attack Calibration (20 minutes)

Clean tone. Play single downstrokes on open E string.
Find the minimum pick attack that produces a full, articulate note. That is your baseline.
Now play your hardest acoustic-calibrated attack. Hear the difference — that's your over-attack margin.
Practice landing at 70% of your acoustic attack. This is your electric default.
Run "In a Big Country" intro pattern at this reduced attack. It should feel lighter but sound fuller.
DAY 3 — Muting Introduction (25 minutes)

Add light gain (see Katana settings below). Now you can hear everything you don't mute.
Practice left-hand muting: when you release a chord before transitioning, lay your fingers lightly across strings.
Practice palm muting: right hand rests lightly on strings near the bridge during and between chords.
Run "Shout" verse pattern. Every chord release should be muted. The noise between chords should be zero.
This will feel clumsy. That's correct. It becomes automatic in about a week.
DAY 4–5 — Setlist Songs at Low Gain (30 minutes each)

Light crunch tone. Run the five easiest setlist songs start to finish.
Do not try to sound heavy yet. You are calibrating feel, not chasing tone.
Notice: Where do your transitions feel sticky? Where does noise appear between chords?
These are your focus points for Week 2.
By the end of Week 1, the electric should feel lighter, more responsive, and weirdly fragile compared to the acoustic. That "fragile" feeling is actually precision. Your acoustic was a blunt instrument; the electric rewards accuracy. You are not weaker — you are more precise. That shift in perception is the whole point of Week 1.

TONE CALIBRATION RULES — NEVER SKIP THESE

  • Always calibrate tone in band context, not alone. What sounds great in your bedroom turns into a muffled mid-range soup when bass, drums, and another guitarist fill the frequency range. Every setting above is a starting point, not gospel.
  • Rhythm guitar cuts through mids, not bass. The beginner instinct is to boost bass for "heaviness." The bass guitar already owns that frequency range. If you boost bass, you fight your bassist and lose. Cut bass, push mids. You will sound heavier by taking up less low-end space.
  • Match gain to your worst song, not your best. If "This Corrosion" needs gain 6 and "Broken Wings" needs gain 3, you're switching channels. Set up your two channels at the extremes of your actual range.
  • Get your sound at rehearsal volume. The Katana 100W at performance volume will sound different from bedroom volume. The EQ shifts at higher SPL. Always set tone during full-band rehearsal, not the night before.
  • Boss Tone Studio / Katana patch memory: Save your two channel settings as a patch. Back it up. The worst possible moment to realize your settings are gone is 15 minutes before soundcheck.
// THE SCOOPED MIDS TRAP

The most common amateur mistake with a metal-adjacent tone is the "scooped mids" setting: bass high, mids low, treble high. This sounds absolutely incredible at bedroom volume. In a band mix, it disappears completely. You will be inaudible. The bass covers your low end, the lead guitar covers your high end, and the midrange hole where you live becomes a void. Never scoop your mids in a band context. Your middle knob should be at or above 5 at all times.

FULL TEMPO DEVELOPMENT PROTOCOL

You're at 75% tempo on all 13 songs. Full tempo is the target. The distance between 75% and 100% is shorter than it sounds — but it has to be covered systematically, not by just playing faster and hoping for the best. Here's the protocol.

THE TEMPO ACCELERATION RULE

// NON-NEGOTIABLE TEMPO METHODOLOGY

Increase tempo in 5% increments only. Play a song cleanly at current tempo three times in a row before advancing. One mistake resets the run count, not the tempo. This is slower than you want and exactly as fast as it actually works.

75% → 80% → 85% → 90% → 95% → 100% = six increments. At three clean runs per increment with honest self-assessment, that's roughly 2–3 weeks of focused work per song cluster. Plan accordingly.

The tempo tap test: Before every practice session, tap your target tempo on your leg before picking up the guitar. If it feels fast, you're at the right increment. If it feels comfortable, you're underestimating your own ability — try the next increment.

SONG TEMPO CLUSTER APPROACH

Group songs by similarity of tempo challenge rather than working them alphabetically. Songs in the same cluster share tempo advancement sessions.

Cluster Songs Challenge Profile Priority Order
A — GROOVE Into the Groove, Tainted Love, True Faith, Suburbia Rhythmic precision, lock with drums, steady eighth-note subdivision. Tempo is feel-based. 1 — Lock the groove first. Everything else follows.
B — DRIVING In a Big Country, Shout, The Whole of the Moon, This Corrosion High-energy sustained performance, pick stamina, right-hand endurance under tempo pressure. 2 — Address after groove is solid.
C — ATMOSPHERIC Last Night I Dreamt, Under the Milky Way, Only You, Everyday Is Like Sunday Slower tempo but more exposed — every mistake is audible. Emotional accuracy is the challenge. 3 — These songs don't have tempo difficulty; they have exposure difficulty.
D — SPECIFIC Broken Wings (encore) Standalone arrangement. Needs separate tempo work. The encore must be bulletproof. 4 — Last and locked.

MISTAKE RECOVERY DRILLS

Every performer at every level makes mistakes. The professionals aren't the people who never mess up — they're the people whose mistakes are invisible to the audience because recovery is faster than perception. This is a trainable skill. You need to actually practice it.

THE FUNDAMENTAL PRINCIPLE

// THE RECOVERY RULE

When you make a mistake, your only job is to find the next chord on time. Not the missed chord. The next one. The impulse to fix what you played wrong will make you miss what comes next, compounding the error. Abandon the mistake. Find the time. Catch the next chord.

The audience does not know what you were supposed to play. They know if the band stops sounding like a band. Stay with the band and you've recovered.

FIVE RECOVERY TECHNIQUES

// TECHNIQUE 1 — THE SKIP

What it is: You miss a chord, play nothing, and re-enter on the next structural marker (start of next bar, start of chorus, etc.).

When to use it: When you lose your place entirely and have no idea what comes next.

Drill: Practice "This Corrosion" main riff. Every four repetitions, deliberately skip two full bars of playing (hold the guitar, mute strings, count silently). Re-enter on bar 1. Do this until re-entry feels smooth, not panicked.

Silence is not a mistake. Silence followed by a confident re-entry is control. Silence followed by a frantic attempted recovery that crashes the chord — that's the problem. Train the silence-to-entry moment.
// TECHNIQUE 2 — THE POWER CHORD FALLBACK

What it is: When you can't remember the full voicing, reduce to a two-string power chord on the root. Root + fifth. Always rhythmically correct. Always passable.

When to use it: When you blank on a chord mid-song but you know where the root is.

Drill: Run "In a Big Country" with full chord voicings. Every time you're about to hit a chord, randomly call out "power chord only" (or have someone else call it). You must land a power chord without slowing down. Vary which chords get the call.

Power chords are not inferior. They are incomplete. In a live context with a full band at volume, a clean power chord is indistinguishable from a full voicing to anyone except a guitar player standing directly behind you. Use this.
// TECHNIQUE 3 — THE MUTE HOLD

What it is: Mute all strings and play the rhythm pattern without sounding notes. You're holding rhythmic position without adding harmonic content.

When to use it: When you need a bar or two to find your place without dropping out of the groove entirely.

Drill: Run any setlist song. At random intervals, mute all strings and maintain rhythm for two full bars. Find your re-entry point. Unmute. Continue. The rhythm should be continuous — no one should hear you drop out.

The drummer and bassist hold the groove. You can briefly be a percussion instrument while you find the map. This is the most invisible recovery technique available to a rhythm guitarist.
// TECHNIQUE 4 — THE ANCHOR NOTE

What it is: When lost in a progression, find the root of the key and play that single note in rhythm until you relocate the chord progression.

When to use it: When you lose track of the progression but can still hear the key.

Drill: Run "Shout." Have a bandmate or recording change the chord unexpectedly on you (play the wrong chord at a random point). You must find the key root note within two bars and re-enter on the next chord. This sounds harder than it is — your bass background means you hear roots naturally.

Your bass background is an enormous advantage here. Root note recovery is not a fallback — it's bass player intuition applied to guitar context. Trust it.
// TECHNIQUE 5 — THE STRUCTURAL LANDMARK

What it is: Mentally mark the structural landmarks of every song: verse start, chorus start, bridge start, outro. When lost, hold mute and count bars until the next landmark. Re-enter there.

When to use it: When everything goes wrong simultaneously.

Drill: For each setlist song, write down the bar numbers of each structural landmark. Know them cold. Drill: someone calls a landmark mid-song ("CHORUS IN 4!"). You must be at the chorus chord on bar 1 regardless of where you were in the arrangement.

The setlist is 13 songs. Most of them are verse-chorus structures you already know. You have more structural knowledge than you think. The drill is just making it explicitly accessible under pressure.

THE FULL RECOVERY DRILL SESSION (Weekly — 20 minutes)

Once per week, starting Week 3, run the following session:

Pick any three setlist songs. Play them back to back with no breaks.
Have a timer set for random interruptions every 2–5 minutes.
When the timer goes: you must "forget" the next chord — play nothing for one bar, then re-enter using whichever recovery technique fits.
Alternatively: have someone call "MISTAKE" at a random point and you execute a recovery technique.
End the session by playing all three songs straight through without any deliberate errors.

The goal: Recovery feels boring. Not scary. The moment recovery feels boring is the moment you've mastered it.
// SECTION 5

PERFORMANCE PSYCHOLOGY

You've done the work. The songs are in your hands. The electric is in your hands. Now the problem becomes: your own brain at showtime. This is the section most guitar instructors skip because it's uncomfortable to admit how much of performance is not about guitar.

WHAT ADRENALINE ACTUALLY DOES TO YOU

What You'll Experience Why It Happens What To Do
Hands shake or tremor Adrenaline causes fine motor disruption. Entirely normal. Play through it. The shaking attenuates within 30–60 seconds of playing. You need to survive the first chord, not the first song.
Everything feels faster than rehearsal Time perception compresses under adrenaline. The song isn't faster — your perception is accelerated. Anchor to the drummer's kick drum. Not the tempo in your head. The actual kick drum in the room.
You forget parts of songs you know cold Cortisol impairs short-term memory retrieval. The song is still there — your access to it is temporarily blocked. Fall back to structural landmarks. Do not try to recall the specific passage. Play the skeleton of the song until recall returns.
Tunnel vision / narrow focus Threat response narrows perceptual field. The room disappears. Pick one fixed point to look at (not the audience, not your guitar). A monitor, a amp head, a mic stand. Visual anchor stabilizes focus.
Dry mouth, racing heart Classic sympathetic nervous system response. You're not dying. Every performer has this. It means you care. Hydrate before, accept the heart rate.
The critical inner voice gets louder Self-monitoring increases under observation threat. Your inner critic is not useful during performance. It's useful in practice. During performance: execute, don't evaluate. Save the critique for after the show.

THE POCKET — STAYING IN IT UNDER PRESSURE

"Staying in the pocket" means playing in rhythmic lock with the rhythm section without rushing, dragging, or reactive tempo changes in response to what's happening around you. Under adrenaline, beginners rush. Under adrenaline, intermediate players underreact by gripping the tempo too hard and going stiff. You want neither.

// THE POCKET PROTOCOL
  • Your reference is the drummer's kick drum, always. Not your internal metronome. Not the bassist. The kick. The kick is the ground.
  • If you feel rushed, play slightly behind the beat. Not late — behind. Laying back by 20ms is rhythmically correct and feels heavy. Rushing sounds thin and anxious.
  • Breathe on the 1. Literally exhale on beat 1 of every bar. This has two functions: it prevents breath-holding (which causes physical tension) and it physically anchors you to the downbeat.
  • Watch the drummer's kick foot, not their face. The kick is ground truth. Face expressions during performance are not useful data.

WHEN YOUR MIND GOES BLANK

It will happen. At some point during a live performance, your mind will go blank. You will look at your guitar and have no idea what you're supposed to be playing. Here is the precise procedure:

  1. 1
    Do not stop playing. Play a muted rhythm pattern. The groove continues.
  2. 2
    Listen for the root note in the bass. Your ear knows what key you're in even when your brain doesn't.
  3. 3
    Find the chord the bassist is playing. Play that root as a power chord.
  4. 4
    Identify the structural landmark you're in. Verse? Chorus? Hold the power chord until the next landmark.
  5. 5
    Re-enter with the full voicing at the next section change.

Total time from blank to recovered: 4–8 bars. The audience heard nothing wrong. You stayed with the groove. The song continued.

The blank-mind moment feels like drowning. It's not. It's a 10-second access failure in a skill you can execute with your eyes closed. The skill is still there. Your job in that moment is not to panic-retrieve it — it's to hold position until it comes back on its own. It always comes back. Trust the rehearsal.

PRE-SHOW MENTAL PROTOCOL

60 MINUTES BEFORE SHOWTIME:
Run pre-show warm-up (see Section 7). No new information. Nothing you haven't done 50 times. Boring and familiar is the goal.

30 MINUTES BEFORE SHOWTIME:
Run the opening 32 bars of the first song in your head. Not out loud, not on the guitar. In your head. Hear your part clearly. This primes retrieval.

15 MINUTES BEFORE SHOWTIME:
Stop practicing. You're done. Stretching, hydrating, chatting with bandmates. No guitar.

5 MINUTES BEFORE SHOWTIME:
Make sure your guitar is in tune. That's the only technical task. One job.

AT SHOWTIME:
The first chord is the hardest one. After the first chord, it's just rehearsal with an audience.

PRE-SHOW WARM-UP ROUTINE

You trained on acoustic. You're performing on electric. Your hands need to make that transition every single show. The pre-show warm-up is where that happens, and it's not optional.

// CRITICAL — THE ACOUSTIC-TO-ELECTRIC TRANSITION EACH SHOW

Even after months of electric practice, you will notice some acoustic-to-electric adjustment time at each show if you've been practicing on acoustic during the week. This is normal and manageable. The pre-show warm-up accounts for it explicitly. Do not try to bypass it by "just playing the electric more" — the acoustic practice is the source of your fretting hand strength. Keep it. Manage the transition.

THE 20-MINUTE PRE-SHOW ROUTINE

MINUTES 0–5
PHYSICAL PREP — NO GUITAR

Hands: Run both hands under warm water for 60 seconds. Cold hands cause tension. Warm hands move freely.

Wrist rolls: 10 slow circles each direction per wrist. Controlled, full range of motion. Not rushed.

Finger stretches: Spread fingers wide, hold 5 seconds. Interlace fingers and push palms outward. Repeat 3x.

Forearm stretch: Extend arm, pull fingers back gently with opposite hand. Hold 10 seconds per arm. This is the one that prevents repetitive strain injury.

Shake out: Shake both hands loosely at the wrist for 10 seconds. Fingers should flap. If they don't, you're still tense.

MINUTES 5–10
FRETTING HAND RECALIBRATION — ELECTRIC, CLEAN TONE

Open chords — pressure calibration: Play E major. Hold it. Now release fretting pressure until the notes start to buzz slightly. Add just enough pressure back to stop the buzz. That is your correct pressure for this guitar tonight. Not acoustic pressure. Not maximum pressure. Minimum viable pressure.

Barre chord recalibration: Same exercise with F barre chord. Find the minimum pressure for a clean ring. This is your performance baseline.

One slow run through any setlist song: Any song at 70% tempo. Not to practice — to feel the electric under your hands again. Notice what's different from your acoustic. Adjust consciously.

Power chords: Run through the power chord positions of your three heaviest setlist songs. Root, 5th, octave. Feel the electric under hard attack.

MINUTES 10–17
SETLIST RUN — THREE HIGH-RISK SONGS, FIRST 32 BARS EACH

Choose the three songs in tonight's setlist that you feel least certain about. Play only the first 32 bars of each at performance tempo. Not the whole song — the entrance. The entrance is where nerves peak. Warm up the entrance.

If all songs feel equally solid (this will happen eventually), choose the opener, the song after the opener, and the closer. These are the highest-stakes positions in the set.

This run is not practice. This is priming. You are telling your hands what's coming.

MINUTES 17–20
STOP AND TUNE

Check tuning on every string with the tuner pedal. Do this after the hands are warm — cold hands cause sharp notes due to increased fretting pressure.

Check tone settings: Channel A and Channel B are set where you want them.

Check cable connection: Wiggle the cable at both ends. If it crackles, fix it now.

Put the guitar down. You're done warming up. Walk away from the instrument. You are ready.

SELF-ASSESSMENT RUBRIC — STAGE READINESS PER SONG

Use this rubric to honestly assess each song once per week in solo practice. Band context is separate — this is your internal checkpoint. Be honest. The only person you're lying to if you're not is yourself.

RUBRIC — HOW TO USE IT

For each song, assess all five dimensions. A song is stage-ready only when all five are at the GREEN level. One RED dimension = not ready. One AMBER dimension = conditional (can perform, but it's a risk).

NOT READY
Conscious errors, stopping, searching for chords, tempo drift, cannot recover from interruption
CONDITIONAL
Mostly correct, occasional errors, recovers but slowly, slight tempo instability under pressure
STAGE READY
No conscious errors, automatic execution, recovers fast, tempo steady, could hold a conversation while playing

FIVE DIMENSIONS — PER SONG

Dimension What to Check Not Ready Conditional Stage Ready
1. Chord Accuracy Do all chords ring clean, including barre chords? Buzzing, muffled strings, wrong shapes Mostly clean, 1–2 problem chords All chords clean every time
2. Tempo Stability Does tempo hold under fatigue and adrenaline? Rushes, drags, or varies by more than 5 BPM Mostly holds, tendency to rush at chorus Tempo holds within 2 BPM throughout
3. Full Song Completion Can you play start to finish without stopping? Must stop to think, cannot finish consistently Can finish, may pause at transitions Start to finish, no stops, three consecutive times
4. Recovery Can you recover from a deliberate mistake? Mistake causes cascade failure or stopping Recovers eventually, takes 4+ bars Recovers within 2 bars, groove maintained
5. Automaticity Can you play it while doing something else? (counting aloud, looking around the room) Cannot maintain if attention diverted Partial attention diversion possible Song runs on autopilot — full attention available for bandmates

BAND REHEARSAL STRUCTURE GUIDE

Band rehearsals are not practice. Practice is what you do alone. Rehearsal is where you integrate what you've practiced with other musicians. These are fundamentally different activities and they have different protocols.

PRINCIPLES FOR BAND REHEARSAL

// REHEARSAL IS NOT PRACTICE

Do not use band rehearsal to learn parts. Your parts should be solid before you walk in. Band rehearsal is for locking arrangements, working on transitions, and building ensemble cohesion. If you use band time to figure out your own parts, you are stealing time from every other musician in the room.

You will not always be the most prepared person in the room. Aim to always be at least as prepared as the average.

EARLY REHEARSAL STRUCTURE — WEEKS 4–8

PHASE A
FIRST THREE REHEARSALS — ARRANGEMENT LOCKING

Goal: Every song has an agreed arrangement. Who starts where, how long are sections, where does the song end.

Protocol: Run each song once through. Stop after each song. Ask: "Does the arrangement feel right? Where did it break down?" Fix one thing per stop. Don't rabbit-hole.

Your role: Listen first. The lead guitarist and drummer likely have the arrangement more firmly set. Fit your part to what they're doing before asserting your own interpretation.

Success metric: After three rehearsals, every song has a definitive start, definitive sections, and definitive end. No more question marks.

PHASE B
REHEARSALS 4–8 — SET RUN FOCUS

Goal: Run songs in setlist order. Develop setlist flow, not just individual songs.

Protocol: Run three to four songs without stopping. Note what breaks down. Isolate the specific transition or section. Fix it. Run the sequence again.

Your role: Watch the setlist flow from a guitar-tone perspective. Are you switching channels correctly? Are your volume levels consistent song to song?

Success metric: Can run eight songs consecutively without a restart. Quality may not be perfect — continuity is the first goal.

PHASE C
REHEARSALS 8+ — FULL SET RUN

Goal: Run the full set start to finish, with encore, as it would happen at a show.

Protocol: Start. Play through. Do not stop for mistakes. Treat it like a show. Fix things after.

Your role: Note your mistakes but do not stop the band for them. After the run, say: "I had a moment in [song] at [section] — can we run that transition?" Then fix it efficiently.

Success metric: Two consecutive full set runs without a restart and without any individual song collapsing. This is your performance rehearsal gate.

ARRANGEMENT CONFLICTS — NO EGO PROTOCOL

At some point, someone will want to play something differently than you've learned it. Maybe the lead guitarist changes a part. Maybe the drummer shifts the feel of a song. Maybe the vocalist wants a different dynamic. Here is the protocol:

  1. Default is: adjust and try it. Your learning investment in the original version is not an argument. The band arrangement is what matters. You can always relearn your part.
  2. If you think the original version is better, say so once. Not twice. Not repeatedly. State your view clearly: "I think the original approach works better here because [specific reason]. Worth trying?" If the band disagrees, drop it. This is not a hill to die on.
  3. If a change is genuinely beyond your current ability, say so directly. "I can't do that cleanly yet — give me two solo practice sessions and I'll have it." This is honest and professional. Nodding along and then not being able to execute it at the next rehearsal is not.
  4. After rehearsal, note all arrangement changes and practice them before next rehearsal. This seems obvious. It is frequently not done. Do it.

WHAT TO DO WHEN THE BAND CHANGES AN ARRANGEMENT YOU'VE LEARNED

This will happen. It's not personal. Here's the exact recovery protocol:

Step 1: At the rehearsal where the change is made, write down the change precisely. Bar numbers if possible. Structural description if not. ("Verse chord 3 is now Dm instead of Am. Chorus adds an extra bar before the last chord.")

Step 2: At the end of rehearsal, run the changed section slowly, just you and whoever made the call, to confirm you understand the change correctly.

Step 3: In solo practice before next rehearsal, practice the new version exclusively. Do not practice the old version. You are building the new pattern — the old one will fade.

Step 4: At the next rehearsal, run the changed song early. Don't leave it until late in the session when fatigue is high.

Total relearning time for a single section change: 1–2 solo practice sessions. For a major structural change: 3–4. This is not a crisis.

WEEK BY WEEK SCHEDULE — 10 WEEK CORE PLAN

This schedule is written assuming 5–10 hours of practice per week. The "floor" column is the 2-hour worst case week. The "target" column is the 5-hour standard week. Do not skip the floor plan — it exists because life happens. A bad week with the floor plan is not a lost week.

BAND AVAILABILITY NOTE: Weeks 1–3 are solo-only. Weeks 4–10 assume band rehearsal has started. If band isn't available by Week 4, extend the solo protocol and compress the band-integrated weeks. The solo work is not wasted — it just gives you more headroom when the band comes together.

BAND REHEARSAL
Phase C: Full set runs. Start to finish. No stopping for mistakes. After each run, brief debrief: "What collapsed?" Fix one thing from each run before the next one.

WEEKS 9–10
PERFORMANCE EXECUTION — THE EXIT CRITERIA

Primary objective: Meet all three Phase 4 exit criteria. Full setlist at tempo with band. Recovery from live mistake. Small audience performance.

SOLO PRACTICE
Maintenance only. 30–45 minute sessions. Run 3–4 songs per session. Focus on your weakest songs from the last rubric assessment. No new learning. Consolidation only.

BAND REHEARSAL
Full set runs with deliberate mistake recovery integrated: At a pre-agreed moment, the drummer signals "mistake happened" and you execute a recovery technique live. Do this in rehearsal before you need to do it for real.

Gate check: Exit Criterion 1 — Can the band play all 13 songs at tempo without stopping? If yes, document it. This is the gate you've been building to for months.
LIVE PERFORMANCE
Execute the small audience event. Do not cancel it. Do not postpone it if you feel unready — unreadiness before a show is a feeling, not a fact. If the rubric says green across the board, you're ready.

Post-performance debrief: Write down three things that went well and one thing to fix. In that order. Celebrate appropriately. Phase 4 closes when you walk off that stage.

FLOOR WEEK (2 hrs): Maintenance practice 45m · Band full set run 75m minimum

PHASE 4 EXIT CRITERIA — THE THREE GATES

All three must be met. They are in order of achievement — you cannot meet Gate 3 without having substantially completed Gates 1 and 2. They are not negotiable. They are not approximate. You either pass them or you haven't finished yet.

// GATE 1 — FULL SETLIST AT TEMPO WITH THE BAND

Definition: All 13 songs, played at full target tempo, with other musicians present, start to finish for each song, with no songs collapsing (stopping, restarting, or requiring the band to bail you out).

Evidence required: Band agrees. Lead guitarist, drummer, and bassist all confirm the set is working. This is not a solo judgment call.

What doesn't count: "I can play them alone." "Most of them." "At 95%." All 13. At tempo. With the band. Full stop.

// GATE 2 — RECOVERY FROM MISTAKE WITHOUT SONG COLLAPSE

Definition: During a band rehearsal or performance, a mistake occurs (including a deliberate one triggered by the rehearsal protocol) and you recover within two bars without the song stopping, without another band member having to stop for you, and without the groove dropping out.

Evidence required: This has happened at least once in band context. Not just in solo drill. In context, with the band, with the pressure.

What doesn't count: "I know the techniques." "I did it in solo practice." It must happen in band context. The gate doesn't care about your drills.

// GATE 3 — LIVE PERFORMANCE IN FRONT OF AN AUDIENCE

Definition: At least one song performed in front of at least three humans who are not in your band. An open mic, a house show, a backyard gathering, a hallway of coworkers who got voluntold. The size doesn't matter. The presence of an audience does.

Evidence required: It happened. You were there. You played. You did not die.

What doesn't count: Planning to do it. "We're almost ready." Postponing it because it's nerve-wracking. The nerve-wracking feeling is the entire point of this gate.

  • Gate 1: Full setlist at tempo with band — band agrees ✓
  • Gate 2: Mid-song mistake recovery in band context without collapse ✓
  • Gate 3: Performed in front of at least one small live audience ✓
  • Pre-show warm-up routine practiced and habitual ✓
  • All 13 songs scoring Stage Ready on the 5-dimension rubric ✓
  • Katana Channels A and B dialed in for live use ✓
  • Electric guitar selected and committed to for Phase 4 ✓
  • Band arrangements locked — no open questions ✓
// FINISH LINE

THE HONEST CLOSING ARGUMENT

This is Phase 4 of a program that started when you couldn't reliably clean-ring an F barre chord. You built the foundations. You built the toolkit. You learned 13 songs. You survived all of it.

Phase 4 is the phase that requires the most from you that has nothing to do with guitar technique. It requires showing up to band rehearsals prepared. It requires playing in front of humans while your hands shake. It requires staying in the groove when you forget what comes next. It requires asking your bandmates for honest feedback and acting on it without taking it personally.

The best argument against your plan succeeding at this stage isn't technique — your technique is functional. The most likely cause of Phase 4 failure in 12 months is fear masquerading as "not ready yet." You will feel not ready before every rehearsal, before every run-through, before the performance. That feeling is not data. It's adrenaline doing its job. The way through it is through it.

Book the show. Do the warm-up. Play the first chord. Everything after that is rehearsal with an audience.
