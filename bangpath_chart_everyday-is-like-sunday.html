// The Desolate Song

Everyday Is Like Sunday is Morrissey at his most bleak and his most beautifully orchestrated. The original has sweeping strings, a melancholic vocal, and a very specific sparse guitar feel. Four chords — C, F, G, Am — in C major. The chord chart gives an explicit strumming pattern. The tab reveals the intro is a melodic arpeggiated figure, not strummed chords. The challenge is the same as Only You: the chords are simple, so everything else is on display. What carries this song is restraint, tone, and a desolate gravity that the strumming pattern needs to convey without being aggressive.

// Arrangement Decision — Melodic or Strummed Intro

Option 1 — Melodic approach: Play the arpeggiated figure as tabbed. More faithful to the original. Creates the orchestral atmosphere before the verse settles in. Requires single-note cleanliness and a delicate touch. High exposure — each note is its own event.

Option 2 — Chord strum intro: Start the song strumming a C chord with the main pattern. Simpler, heavier, skips the delicate opening. Loses the song's signature intro atmosphere but is easier to execute cleanly from bar 1.

For a metal arrangement the melodic intro creates contrast — you arrive with delicacy before the verse settles into full chord territory. Discuss with your guitarist. If they're playing a complementary melodic line, the intro can be coordinated between you.

// C Major — The Most Guitar-Friendly Key

C major gives you three open chords out of four — C, G, and Am all use open strings. Only the F barre requires any barre work, and you've been drilling that across most of the setlist. This should be one of the more comfortable songs to play. The chord shapes are all well within your capability at this point. Let the ease of the chords free your attention to focus on feel and tone instead.

// What "Resigned Forward Motion" Means in Practice

The pattern should feel like it's carrying the song rather than driving it. There's a difference between a pattern that pushes forward with energy and one that moves forward with inevitability. This song needs the latter. The downstrokes on beats 1 and 4 should land with weight — not velocity. Think "heavy" not "fast." The up-strokes on 2& and 3& are barely there — just enough to keep the rhythm moving without adding lightness or bounce that would conflict with the song's emotional temperature.

06 //
TECHNIQUE NOTES
THREE THINGS TO NAIL
TECH 01 //
THE EMOTIONAL WEIGHT IN DOWNSTROKES

The central technique for this song: The downstroke on beat 1 of each bar should carry emotional weight without being physically loud. This is the hardest thing to teach because it's about feel, not mechanics. The difference between a downstroke that sounds "resigned" and one that sounds "energetic" is in the deceleration — the stroke slows slightly as it completes rather than snapping through sharply.

How to practice it: Play a C chord with a single downstroke on beat 1. Before you hit the strings, imagine you're signing your name for something you don't want to sign. The stroke should feel heavy and considered, not reflexive. Do this for 5 minutes on a single C chord before adding any pattern or other chords. The feel you develop in that 5 minutes is the emotional foundation of the whole song.

TECH 02 //
THE F BARRE IN CONTEXT — AGAIN

F at fret 1 throughout the whole song: Verse, pre-chorus, chorus, bridge, outro — F is everywhere. By this point in your practice schedule the F barre should be completely automatic from all the other songs that use it. If it isn't, this is the diagnostic song — if F is still giving you trouble here, address the barre technique specifically.

Specific concern: In the verse, the pattern is C → F → C → F with one bar per chord. At 117 BPM that gives you exactly one bar to move from C (open, fingers off the neck) to F (full barre fret 1). The C → F transition needs to happen in the dead space of beat 4 — your fretting hand starts moving during the last beat of the C bar so the F lands clean on beat 1 of the next.

TECH 03 //
DESOLATION DYNAMICS — MAKING SIMPLICITY INTERESTING

The paradox: Four chords, the same pattern for most of the song, 117 BPM for 3+ minutes. Without deliberate dynamic shaping this becomes a flat, mechanical experience. The song's emotional journey needs to be expressed through subtle variation in attack and ring — not through adding complexity that isn't there.

The map: Intro: quiet and considered (60% attack). Verse: slightly more present (65%). Pre-chorus: building toward darkness (75%). Am on "Armageddon": 85% — the heaviest moment before the chorus. Chorus: open and resigned (70% — not triumphant). Bridge: controlled (70%). Post-chorus/outro: gradually building toward the final C (70% rising to 80%). The arc is not loud-to-soft or soft-to-loud — it's controlled-to-momentarily-dark-and-back, twice.

// What Good Execution Sounds Like
The intro creates a grey, oceanic atmosphere — whether melodic or strummed, it should feel like looking at a beach out of season. The verse is bleak and simple: C and F, nothing else, the lyric doing all the emotional work. The pre-chorus builds toward Am's darkness with accumulated weight. "Armageddon come Armageddon!" lands with the heaviest note in the song. The chorus resolves — C G F — like a sigh. "Every day is silent and grey" sounds exactly as grey as the title promises. The bridge's F G alternation accumulates without escalating. The final chorus and outro repeat until the final C chord settles everything into resigned acceptance. The song does not end triumphantly. It ends with certainty — the certainty that tomorrow will also be like Sunday. Play the final chord like you believe that.
