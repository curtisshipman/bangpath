// The Harmonic Complexity Song

True Faith has the most chord vocabulary of any song on the setlist — eight chords compared to Shout's four. The verse alone moves through six different chords. The bass tabs (both EADG tuning, 117 BPM) confirm the key as D minor and the root movement matches the chord chart exactly. The guitar tab confirms the syncopated 16th-note strum pattern that runs throughout. This song rewards time spent learning the chord sequence first before adding any pattern — the map has to be in your head before the hands can do their job.

// The A Major Chord — The Surprise

Every chord in this song is minor or neutral — except one. The A major chord (not Am) appears at the end of each verse, on "Of the things that cost you too much" / "I guess there's just no way of knowing" / "In a world that's so demanding." In a D minor song, A major is the unexpected major chord that creates a brief, dissonant lift before the chorus resolves everything. Do not play Am here — the A major is intentional and it matters. The guitar tab and chord chart both explicitly show A (major) fingering.

// Am vs A — The Critical Distinction

Both Am and A major appear in the verse. They look similar and sit next to each other in the chord progression. Am has fret 1 on the B string — A major has fret 2. One wrong finger and you've played the wrong chord. The A major chord appearing in a minor key song is an intentional harmonic device — it creates a brief, unresolved brightness before the chorus arrives. Playing Am instead flattens the emotional movement. Practice them side by side until the distinction is automatic.

// What Makes This Pattern Work

The two straight downstrokes on beats 1 and 2 (without upstrokes) give the pattern weight and authority. The down-up pairs on beats 3 and 4 give it forward momentum. The pattern feels like it's always slightly ahead of itself — the upstrokes on 3& and 4& pull you toward the next bar. At 117 BPM this creates a relentless, machine-like drive that replaces what the synths do in the original New Order recording.

TECHNIQUE NOTES
FOUR SPECIFIC THINGS
TECH 01 //
VERSE CHORD MAP MEMORISATION

The only way this works: The verse chord sequence needs to be memorised, not read. At 117 BPM with eight different chords you cannot look at a chart and play simultaneously — the chord has to be in your hands before you consciously register needing it.

Method: Run the verse chord sequence without the song. One chord per beat, 60 BPM, no strumming — just single downstrokes. Say the chord name aloud as you play it. Dm. Bb. C. Am. Dm. Bb. C. Am. C. Dm. C. Bb. Am. C. Bb. Am. A. Run it 10 times forward. Then close your eyes and run it from memory. Repeat until you can do it blind at 80 BPM.

TECH 02 //
AM → A MAJOR TRANSITION

The move: Am to A major is one fret higher on the B string only — your ring or middle finger shifts from fret 2 to... actually, for A major you re-finger the chord entirely. Am uses index on B(1), middle on D(2), ring on G(2). A major uses the same shape but at fret 2 for all three middle strings (D, G, B at fret 2), with B string now at fret 2 instead of fret 1.

Simplest fingering for A major: Barre frets 2 on D, G, and B with your ring finger (or lay your middle, ring, and pinky across those three strings). The open A and e strings ring freely.

Drill: Am → A major → Am → A major at 70 BPM. The transition must be clean and the A major must ring fully before moving on. 5 minutes daily until automatic.

TECH 03 //
F BARRE ENDURANCE IN CONTEXT

The problem unique to True Faith: Unlike songs where F appears in the verse, here F is the second chord of every single chorus pass. After a complex verse with six chords, you then have to play the F barre (fret 1, the hardest position) cleanly, repeatedly, in the highest-energy section of the song. Barre fatigue from the verse makes this harder than it would be in isolation.

The fix: Practice verse → chorus back-to-back specifically. Run the full verse chord sequence, then immediately play the F barre on the chorus downbeat. The test is whether the F lands clean after the verse workout, not just in isolation.

TECH 04 //
G/D SLASH CHORD — MUTING THE LOW STRINGS

What G/D requires: You play a G chord shape (frets 3 on e and B, open G and D) while muting the A string and the low E string. The open D string becomes the bass note. If you accidentally hit the A string (fret 2 in a standard G chord), you add an unwanted A note. The A string must be completely silent.

Muting technique: Let the tip of your middle finger (which frets A fret 2 in a standard G) lightly touch the low E without pressing. Shift your A string muting to the fretting hand's index finger touching lightly across the A string. Strum the middle four strings (D through e) only — restrict your strum range to avoid the low E entirely.

// What Good Execution Sounds Like
The intro locks the pattern immediately — the audience knows within four bars that this has a specific rhythmic personality. The verse has a restless, searching quality as the chords move through their sequence — it should never feel like you're scrambling, even though you have six chords to navigate. The Am → A major shift at the end of each verse creates a brief, unexpected brightness that makes the chorus arrival feel like relief. The chorus is simple and wide — four chords, full sound, the pattern unchanged but the emotional space much larger. The bridge's G/D creates a floating, uncertain quality — the bass note D anchors while the G chord floats. The outro's G chord repeats with increasing conviction until the final cut. At no point does the rhythmic pattern waver. 117 BPM throughout, consistent, locked.
