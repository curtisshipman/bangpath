01 // OVERVIEW THE BIG PICTURE
// Mission Brief

Mr. Mister's Broken Wings is a power ballad built on a hypnotic, floating arpeggio figure in the original. The heavy metal arrangement for !bang!path! replaces that arpeggiated synth/guitar texture with chord stabs and a locked rhythmic groove that preserves the emotional weight without losing the song's dreamy, suspended quality. This is your first encore — the crowd is warm, the energy is there. Play it like a ballad with a metal backbone, not a thrash track with a pretty melody over it. The sus2 chords throughout the verse are the signature sound — honor them.

// ⚠ The G#sus2 at Fret 11 Problem

G#sus2 is voiced at the 11th fret. This is the highest barre position in the entire setlist. At the 11th fret, the fret spacing is narrower — this is actually easier physically than fret 1–3, but it's psychologically terrifying if you haven't drilled it. The shape itself is simple (just a sus2 barre), but the position will feel unfamiliar. Drill it isolated before integrating. The chord appears in verse sections and transitions — you'll need to land it cleanly at 99 BPM.

08 // TECHNIQUE NOTES
01
// THE FRET 4–TO–11 JUMP
The C#sus2 (fret 4) to G#sus2 (fret 11) jump is the hardest transition in this song and the longest position shift in the set. The chord shape is identical — it's purely a position shift. Drill it slowly: play C#sus2 for 2 bars, then on bar 3 beat 1 land G#sus2 at fret 11. At tempo, you need to begin moving your fretting hand during the last beat of the C#sus2 bar. The shape forms in transit. Trust it.
02
// SUS2 vs MINOR — SAME FRET TRAP
C#sus2 (fret 4) and C#m (fret 4) are both at fret 4 but completely different shapes. C#sus2 is a sus2 barre (1-1-1-3-1 fingering), C#m is a minor Am-style barre (1-1-1-3-4). In the verse-to-chorus transition, you land from G#sus2 at fret 11 down to C#m at fret 4 — NOT C#sus2. The minor shape must be ready. Drill the G#sus2 → C#m transition specifically as its own exercise.
03
// DYNAMIC SHAPING — METAL BALLAD
This is an encore, not a main-set track. The crowd needs a moment to breathe after the setlist. Play the intro and verses quieter than you think is right. The chorus should lift significantly — that's the emotional payoff. The bridge is the softest point of the whole song. The final chorus comes back loud. This dynamic architecture is what makes the song work in a metal context. Flatline volume = flatline emotion.
04
// LETTING SUS2 CHORDS RING
The sus2 chords in the verse should ring longer than you'd expect for a metal arrangement. The original arpeggiates them — your strummed version needs to compensate by letting the chord sustain between strums. Reduce muting significantly in the verse. The suspended quality of D#sus2/C#sus2/G#sus2 comes from the unresolved 2nd — if you kill the ring, you kill the suspended feeling. Let it breathe.
09 // FEEL DESCRIPTION
// THE TARGET STATE
You're opening the encore with a power ballad. The room has just roared through the main set and you're giving them something that cuts through with emotion instead of volume. Verse: floating, suspended, slightly haunted. Chorus: wide open, soaring — this is the part everyone in the room will sing. Bridge: intimate, almost conversational. Final outro: the lights go down, the last chord decays, and everyone is very, very still for about three seconds. That silence is the best sound in your set. Play toward it.

// Priority Drill

Before running full songs at any tempo, isolate the three hardest transitions: (1) C#sus2→G#sus2 (fret 4 to fret 11) (2) G#sus2→C#m (fret 11 to fret 4, different shape on arrival) (3) C#sus2 shape vs C#m shape (same fret, different shape).
