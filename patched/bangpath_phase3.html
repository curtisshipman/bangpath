ACOUSTIC PRIMARY
EXIT CRITERIA: NON-NEGOTIABLE

You passed Phase 2. Congratulations. You built the tools. Chord voicings. Strumming patterns. Left-hand embellishment. Barre chord transitions. You practiced them in isolation, you got reps on individual skills, and you cleared the exit criteria.

Phase 3 does not care about your skills in isolation.

Phase 3 is 13 songs. Real structures. Real tempo. Real stamina demands. You will play things that sound bad before they sound good. You will discover that knowing a chord and playing it in context while maintaining time and feel are not the same thing. This is the phase where intermediate guitarists separate from people who "kind of play guitar."

You are not here to master these songs. You are here to make them not scary. There is a meaningful difference. Mastery is Phase 4. Phase 3 exit criteria are: 75% tempo, start to finish, no panic, 4–5 songs in a row without collapse. That is it. That is enough.

Do not skip ahead. Do not rush songs to "done" before the self-assessment rubric clears. Do not lie to yourself about what 75% tempo actually sounds like.

Let's go.

EXIT CRITERIA

// NON-NEGOTIABLE GATES

These criteria are gates, not guidelines. "Mostly there" is not "there." The rubric at the end of each song tells you exactly what done looks like. Use it. Be honest.

TIER SYSTEM

// HOW THIS PHASE IS STRUCTURED

Songs are organized into three tiers based on your specific profile — acoustic primary, barre chords reliable but not effortless, left-hand embellishment functional. You will not work on one song at a time. By Week 4 you are running multiple songs in every session. Song-switching fatigue is a real thing you need to build resistance to.

TIER 1
WEEKS 1–3 · ENTRY POINTS
Learn these first. Build confidence and calibrate your song-learning process. Simpler chord sets, more familiar progressions.
TIER 2
WEEKS 3–7 · CORE REPERTOIRE
The bulk of the setlist. These get the most calendar real estate. New technical demands introduced progressively.
TIER 3
WEEKS 6–10 · BOSS FIGHTS
Songs with the most technical or stamina demands. Approached after the others are partially embedded. F#m barre, drop D, full-set closing.

// Left-Hand Embellishment — Pivot Fingering

The G → Cadd9 transition is one of the cleanest in guitar. Your ring and pinky fingers on the 3rd fret of the B and high E strings do not move between G and Cadd9. Only index and middle move. This is a two-finger transition, not a full-hand reset. Exploit it.

G = 3-2-0-0-0-3 (pinky + ring planted on B and high E) Cadd9 = x-3-2-0-3-3 (same pinky + ring — they don't move) The pivot: index and middle only move to cover the C bass notes.

STAMINA BUILDING PROTOCOL

// FOUR PILLARS

Your right hand is a muscle. Your left hand is a muscle. Your focus is a muscle. All three need progressive overload, not just technical drilling.

PILLAR 1 — RIGHT-HAND ENDURANCE

Pattern A (driving eighths) at tempo for 4 minutes per song, back to back, is a forearm endurance challenge. Target it directly rather than hoping it builds through song drilling alone.

// WEEKLY TABATA DRILL (add from Week 4)

20 seconds driving eighths on any chord at target tempo. 10 seconds rest. Repeat 8 rounds. Total: 4 minutes. Do this 2× per week at the START of a session. Track: can you maintain pick attack quality through round 8? When yes, increase tempo by 4 BPM.

PILLAR 2 — LEFT-HAND BARRE ENDURANCE

Barre chords are harder on acoustic. They will cause fatigue before open chords do. This is the limiting factor in multi-song runs. Build resistance directly.

// BARRE CHORD LADDER (from Week 5)

Play each barre chord for 30 seconds of driving eighths, then shift to the next without a break:

F (133211) → Bm (x24432) → F#m (244222) → Gm (355333) → Bb (x13331)

Total: 2.5 minutes continuous barre playing. 2× per week. Track: clean ring through all five? First week you probably can't. Build toward it.

// QUALITY COLLAPSE MONITORING

After each multi-song run, rate 1–5: (1) right-hand pick attack last vs first song; (2) left-hand barre clarity last vs first; (3) chord transition accuracy last vs first. A drop of 2+ points means you've hit your stamina ceiling. End the run. Note where it broke. This is diagnostic information, not failure.

PILLAR 4 — FOCUS AND FORM MAINTENANCE

After 30+ minutes of playing, your focus drifts. When focus drifts, time drifts. When time drifts, quality collapses before your hands do.

// FOCUS DRILL

Play any chord progression with a metronome for 5 minutes. Think about nothing except the metronome click. Every time you realize you've drifted — a thought about dinner, whether that chord rang cleanly, anything — mark it mentally. At the end of 5 minutes, note how many times you drifted. This number should decrease over weeks.

// Global Exit Criteria Check
  • Cold start test: Pick any song from the list without preparation. Start playing it. Do you know the chord sequence? Do you start without significant hesitation? Test across any random three songs. If yes across all three: checked.
  • Consecutive run test: Play 4–5 songs in a row without stopping between them. In the 5th song, is your right-hand pick attack still clean? Are barre chords ringing? Are chord transitions accurate? If yes: checked.
  • Intimidation test: Look at the full song list. Is any song causing a sinking feeling? If yes: that song is not phase-complete and you have specific work to do. If no: checked.
  • Tempo honesty test: 75% target is not "I played it once at that tempo." It is "I can run this song at that tempo three times in a row and the third run is as clean as the first." Apply this standard without negotiation.

Phase 3 is where most self-taught guitarists discover the gap between "I know this song" and "I can play this song." They are different things. Knowing a song means you've seen the chord chart and played through it a few times. Playing a song means you can pick it up cold, run it at tempo, and not fall apart when something unexpected happens.

The schedule above is structured to close that gap through repetition, progressive tempo, and multi-song runs. None of it is exciting. It is all necessary.

The songs in this setlist are genuinely good songs. When you're running Tainted Love at 90 BPM and it sounds like a machine and you're not thinking about the chords anymore — that is the feeling this phase is building toward.

Phase 4 is the electric transition, the performance prep, the full setlist runs. To get there, you have to be here first. Do the work. Clear the rubric. Be honest with yourself about what's done and what isn't.
