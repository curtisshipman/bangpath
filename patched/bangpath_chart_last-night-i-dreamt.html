// Two Songs in One — Key Changes + Time Signature

This is the most structurally unusual song on the setlist. The piano intro (bars 1–32) is in C minor with 12 chords including complex barre voicings like Abmaj7, Bb/F, and Dsus2. Then the verses modulate to E minor / G major — a completely different key — and simplify to just four chords: Em, C, Cm, B.

The second major challenge: 12/8 time signature. This is compound triple meter — not the standard 4/4 of every other song on the setlist. Each bar has four groups of three 8th notes instead of four straight beats. The strumming feel is fundamentally different. You need to internalise 12/8 before you can play this song correctly.

The easiest way to feel 12/8: think of it as a slow shuffle or a ballad with a triplet feel. If you've heard "With or Without You" (U2) or "Behind Blue Eyes" (The Who) — that's 12/8. It has a rolling, wave-like quality. The Smiths original has a slow orchestral sweep that comes entirely from this time signature. In your metal arrangement the same feel applies — don't try to force it into 4/4.

THE PATTERN IN 12/8
FEEL THE TRIPLET FIRST · THEN ADD CHORDS
12/8 BALLAD STRUM — ROLLING WAVE
FEEL: Slow · Orchestral · Restrained · Every hit has weight · Nothing is rushed

In 12/8 the natural strum approach is to let each chord breathe across its three subdivisions. A simple but effective rhythm: downstroke on beat 1 of each group, let it ring across the "and-a," then decide whether to strum again on beat 2 or hold. The song is slow enough that full ringing is appropriate — this is not a stab-pattern song.

// How to Practice 12/8 Feel

Don't count "1 and 2 and" — count "1 and a, 2 and a, 3 and a, 4 and a" aloud. Three syllables per beat group. Tap your foot on the four main beats. The extra two subdivisions per beat are what create the rolling, wave-like feel. Set the metronome to 50 BPM and count triplets against it before picking up the guitar. Get the feel in your body first.

// Cm in the Verse — The Linking Chord

The Cm barre chord appears in both the intro (C minor key) and the verses (E minor key). In the verse it's a borrowed chord — a minor chord from outside the E minor scale that creates darkness and tension before resolving. It's the same physical chord shape in both sections. Learn it once, use it in both contexts. The fact that it crosses the key modulation makes it a structural linchpin of the song.

// Band Arrangement Decision Required

The original intro is a long orchestral build — piano, strings, building over 30+ bars before the vocal enters. In a live metal arrangement you need to decide: do you play the full intro, a shortened version, or does the band start on the verse and skip the intro entirely? This decision should be made before you spend significant time learning all 8 intro chord lines. If you cut the intro, you only need to learn 4 verse chords. Talk to the band first.

SECTION CHARTS

Execution philosophy for the intro: This is not a rhythm guitar showcase. It's support for the orchestral swell. Play each chord with a single controlled downstroke, let it ring across all 12 eighth notes in the bar, and get out of the way. The intro should sound full but restrained — if you're hearing your guitar specifically, you're playing too hard. This section is about creating atmosphere, not demonstrating technique. Restraint is the technique.
VERSE
Em key · Em C Cm B cycling · vocal-led · 4 chords only
Per line Em → C · one chord per bar · Em on "Last night I dreamt" · C on "that somebody loved me"
Darker lines Cm → B · Cm arrives like a shadow · B resolves with tension "No hope, no harm / Just another false alarm"
V2 variation C → Cm → B → Em · chord order shifts slightly in verse 2 · same chords, different sequence Both verses
The verse dynamic: Em and C are open chords — wide, resonant, vulnerable. Cm and B are barre chords — closed, tense, claustrophobic. The song alternates between these two emotional states in every verse. The open chords should ring freely and fully. The barre chords should feel like a fist closing. Play them with different character, not just different notes.
OUTRO
C Cm B Em repeating · "It goes on" · build and end hard
Pattern C – Cm – B – Em · repeating · increase intensity each pass
End Em · decisive final Em · hold · cut
The outro build: Each repeat of C → Cm → B → Em should feel slightly more inevitable than the last. The original fades — your version doesn't. The final Em should arrive with the full weight of everything that came before it. Play it like a verdict, not a suggestion.
07 //
TECHNIQUE NOTES
FOUR THINGS TO MASTER
TECH 01 //
INTERNALISING 12/8

The only way: Listen to the original recording and count along — "1-and-a, 2-and-a, 3-and-a, 4-and-a" — until the triplet groupings feel natural. Do this for 5 minutes before ever picking up the guitar for this song. 12/8 is a feel, not a formula. If you try to count your way through it while playing, you'll tighten up. The goal is to absorb the rolling, wave-like quality until it's automatic.

Reference songs in 12/8: U2's "With or Without You," The Who's "Behind Blue Eyes," Elvis Presley's "Can't Help Falling in Love." All share this same compound triple feel. Listen to any of them and count along to calibrate your ear before tackling this one.

TECH 02 //
THE KEY MODULATION MOMENT

The transition: The intro's final chord (C or Bb/F) resolves into the verse's Em. This is a key change — you're moving from C minor harmony to E minor harmony. The Em chord itself bridges both worlds (it exists in both keys), so the modulation can be smooth if the Em arrives with the right character — open, resonant, a release after the intro's density.

Practice the joint: Play the last two bars of the intro (Bb/F → C), then land the Em verse chord on beat 1 of the next bar. Repeat this transition 20 times until the key change feels completely natural. The Em should arrive like relief, not like an abrupt stop.

TECH 03 //
ABMAJ7 — THE UNUSUAL INTRO CHORD

Why it's tricky: Abmaj7 at fret 4 is an unusual partial barre voicing — index finger covers A, B, and G strings at fret 4 while the ring finger frets D at fret 5. The high E string is muted. This chord appears three times in the opening intro sequence (lines 1-3) — it's the second chord of every line and needs to be clean before anything else.

Drill: Cm → Abmaj7 → Am → Bb/F at 50 BPM in 12/8. One chord per bar. The Abmaj7 should ring fully on all four fretted strings. If the G string is buzzing, your index finger isn't making proper contact at fret 4. Adjust the finger angle.

TECH 04 //
RESTRAINT AS THE PRIMARY TECHNIQUE

The central challenge of this song: Almost every other song on the setlist rewards playing harder, driving more, locking in tighter. This song requires the opposite. The Smiths original is slow, orchestral, and emotionally devastated. Playing this song with too much attack or too busy a pattern destroys it.

The test: Record yourself playing one verse. If you can hear yourself thinking "I'm really playing guitar here," you're playing too much. The guitar should feel like it's barely there — a suggestion of harmony underneath Morrissey's vocal. Every chord should ring, not stab. Every transition should be smooth, not punctuated. This is the song where less is always more.

// What Good Execution Sounds Like
The intro moves like a tide — slow, inevitable, each chord dissolving into the next. The 12/8 feel creates a rolling quality that nothing with a straight 4/4 pattern can replicate. The key modulation into Em arrives like daylight after a long darkness — the open strings ring wide where the intro was closed and chromatic. The verse is spacious: Em and C breathe fully, Cm and B compress and tense, the cycle repeats with accumulating weight. "It goes on" in the outro becomes more true with each repetition. The final Em is not triumphant — it's exhausted and certain. In a metal arrangement this song doesn't need to be loud to be heavy. It needs to be deliberate, controlled, and slow. The weight comes from restraint, not volume.
