// The Most Exposed Song on the Setlist

Four chords. The whole song. A major is one of the easiest guitar keys. The chord shapes are all basic. By every technical metric this is the simplest song on the setlist. And it is also the most dangerous one to play live. The original Yazoo recording is sparse, intimate, and built around Alison Moyet's vocal. There is almost nothing else happening. In your arrangement, every note you play is audible. Every transition is heard. Every chord that buzzes or dies early is exposed.

The challenge is not the chords. The challenge is making four simple chords sound deliberate, controlled, and intentional for 3+ minutes in front of an audience with nowhere to hide.

WHAT THE TAB REVEALS
THE ORIGINAL GUITAR PART IS MELODIC · NOT STRUMMED
// Tab Analysis — B String Melodic Run

The guitar tab shows that the original Yazoo verse part is not chord strumming — it's a single-note melodic line on the B string running 0-2-2-2-3-2-0 alongside the chord changes. This is the guitar replacing/doubling the synth arpeggio that defines the song. In your metal arrangement you have two options: keep the melodic single-note approach (more faithful, more exposed, more interesting) or play full chords with the strumming pattern from the chord chart (simpler, heavier, more metal). Both are valid. Decide with your guitarist which approach suits the arrangement.

// Arrangement Decision — Melodic vs Full Chords

Option 1 — Melodic approach (tab version): Play the single-note B string line in the verse. Requires clean single-note technique and confident tone. More faithful to the original. Maximum exposure — every note is its own event. This is the harder option technically but the more interesting one musically.

Option 2 — Full chord approach (chord chart): Play full A and E chords with the strumming pattern throughout the verse. Heavier, simpler, more "metal." Loses some of the original song's intimacy but gains weight. Easier to execute confidently at performance level.

Both options use the same chord vocabulary. The verses are the only section where this choice matters — chorus and bridge are full chords regardless.

// The F#m Relief

F#m at fret 2 is actually one of the more comfortable barre positions on the guitar — fret 2 has more flexibility than fret 1 (the hardest position). The E-shape barre at fret 2 should feel significantly easier than the F barre you've been drilling for other songs. If F#m is giving you trouble after working through the other setlist songs, something is wrong with your barre technique generally, not with this specific chord.

// Attack Level for This Song

This pattern at moderate attack is very different from the same pattern played hard. Only You needs a softer touch than Shout or In a Big Country. The pick attack should be perhaps 60-70% of what you'd use on a driving song. The chords should ring, not punch. If your pick is leaving pick marks on the strings or making a "thwacking" sound, you're hitting too hard. This song rewards a light, controlled touch where every string resonates cleanly.

The intro is an audition: Four bars of A chord before the vocal enters. The audience hears your tone, your intonation, and your control before any complexity arrives. A buzzing A chord in the intro tells the audience this is going to be rough. A clean, ringing A chord tells them everything is under control. Nail the intro and the rest of the song will feel easier. Flub it and you'll be chasing your confidence for the next three minutes.

06 //
TECHNIQUE NOTES
THREE THINGS TO NAIL
TECH 01 //
TONE QUALITY — THE ONLY REAL CHALLENGE

What this song actually tests: Every other song on the setlist has enough complexity that the audience's attention is on the arrangement, the tempo, the chord movement. Only You has none of that. What the audience hears is your tone — the actual quality of your guitar sound, the sustain of your chords, the clarity of your notes.

What good tone requires here: Chords fully fretted, all strings ringing cleanly. No muted strings. No dead notes. Pick attack light enough that you're not causing string buzz from impact. If you have the Katana, use the acoustic simulation setting or clean with just enough warmth — this song doesn't need gain to work. It may sound better with less gain than anything else in the set.

Test: Play an A chord and let it ring for 4 full seconds. Listen to every string. All six should still be audible and ringing at the 3-second mark. If any string dies early, something is muted or fretted incorrectly.

TECH 02 //
B STRING MELODIC RUN — IF USING THE TAB APPROACH

The technique: Single-note picking on the B string, frets 0-2-2-2-3-2-0. Each note needs to ring clearly before the next one arrives. This is not fast — at 106 BPM there's one note roughly every half-beat. The challenge is tone, not speed.

Common mistakes: Muting adjacent strings accidentally with the fretting hand. Picking so hard that the note has an unwanted attack click. Not letting each note sustain before moving to the next. The run should sound like a singing melodic line, not a series of separate plucked events.

Drill: Play the run at 60 BPM with a metronome. One note per beat. Listen to the sustain on each note before you pick the next. Increase tempo by 4 BPM when the line sounds musical rather than mechanical.

TECH 03 //
DYNAMIC CONTROL — THE THREE-MINUTE LONG GAME

The problem with simple songs: When there are only four chords and a straightforward pattern, the temptation is to play every bar with the same attack and energy. The result is a flat, undynamic performance that sounds like a practice session rather than a concert.

Map the dynamics before you play: Verse = 60% attack, intimate. Chorus = 80% attack, more open. Bridge = 70% attack, building. Final chorus = 85% attack, full conviction. These numbers don't need to be literal — but you need a conscious plan for how the energy moves through the song. If you walk in without a plan, every bar will sound the same.

Rule of thumb: Each chorus should feel slightly bigger than the one before it. The final "Only you" should be the most certain, most resolved moment in the entire song.

// What Good Execution Sounds Like
The intro A chord rings wide and certain — the audience knows immediately what kind of song this is going to be. The verse is intimate: each chord change is smooth, the transitions inaudible, the melodic line (if used) singing clearly above the chord bed. The F#m barre arrives without drama, sounds like it belongs, resolves back to E naturally. The chorus opens — D to E to A to D, building toward "Only you" — and the A resolution lands like a held breath finally released. The bridge has the most harmonic movement of any section and should feel like the emotional centre of the song before the final chorus settles everything. The outro A chord rings and rings until the band cuts it. The simplest song on the setlist, played with total control and conviction. That's the hardest thing to do.
