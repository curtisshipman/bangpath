// The Atmospheric Song

Under the Milky Way is the dreamiest, most open song on the setlist. At 68 BPM it is significantly slower than everything else — almost half the tempo of True Faith or In a Big Country. The chord vocabulary includes two unusual shapes: Fmaj7 (an open major 7th that gives the song its characteristic shimmer) and Asus4/F# (a slash chord where you play an Asus4 shape with F# in the bass). The tab confirms both sources: the guitar part is predominantly Am → Asus4 hammer-on movement, repeated throughout every section.

The Am → Asus4 back-and-forth is the defining motif of this song — the guitar doesn't just strum chord blocks, it oscillates between Am and the suspended 4th above it. This is an embellishment technique, not just chord strumming.

THE AM → ASUS4 MOTIF
THE DEFINING TECHNIQUE OF THIS SONG
// What the Tab Reveals

Both guitar tabs show the same thing throughout the verse: the guitar oscillates between Am (frets 0-1-2-2-2-0) and Asus4 (frets 0-0-2-2-3-0) — the only difference is the pinky adding fret 3 on the B string for the Asus4, then lifting for the Am. This creates the suspended → resolved oscillation that defines the song's dreamy quality. It is not just two separate chords being strummed — it is a continuous back-and-forth hammer-on/pull-off motion within the Am chord shape. The tab shows this happening within single bars, not at chord change boundaries.

// Fmaj7 — The Song's Signature Colour

Fmaj7 is the chord that makes Under the Milky Way sound like Under the Milky Way. The open e string ringing against the fretted B-G-D strings creates a shimmering, unresolved quality that no other chord can replicate here. The voicing (x-x-3-2-1-0) is not a barre — it's a partial chord using only four strings. Let the open high e ring freely every time. If you mute it the chord loses its characteristic sound entirely.

// The Am → Asus4 and the Pattern — How They Combine

The hammer-on Am → Asus4 motion and the strumming pattern are not separate things — they happen simultaneously. While the pick arm executes the pattern above, the pinky hammers and pulls off on the B string within those strums. So a single bar might sound like: strum Am · hammer to Asus4 · let ring · strum Am · pull off from Asus4 · strum Asus4 · strum Am. The pattern and the motif weave together. Practice the hammer-on motion first in isolation, then add the strumming pattern around it.

06 //
TECHNIQUE NOTES
THREE THINGS TO MASTER
TECH 01 //
THE AM → ASUS4 HAMMER-ON

The mechanics: Hold the Am shape (index on B fret 1, middle on D fret 2, ring on G fret 2). Without moving any other finger, hammer your pinky down onto B string fret 3 to create Asus4. Pull the pinky off — the B string returns to fret 1 (index is still there). This is a smooth, continuous motion. The pick arm strum happens around this pinky motion, not in coordination with it.

What it should sound like: A gentle wavering between minor and suspended — like the chord is inhaling and exhaling. Not aggressive hammer-ons. Not loud pull-offs. Subtle oscillation that creates the floating quality without being technically flashy.

Drill: Hold Am. Hammer pinky to B fret 3. Pull off. Repeat continuously at 60 BPM — one hammer, one pull-off per beat. The motion should become completely unconscious before you add the strumming pattern around it. 5 minutes daily.

TECH 02 //
FMAJ7 OPEN E STRING — KEEP IT RINGING

The chord shape: Fmaj7 is x-x-3-2-1-0 — ring finger on D fret 3, middle on G fret 2, index on B fret 1, open high e. Only four strings are played. The low E and A strings are muted. The open high e string is essential — it's the major 7th interval that gives the chord its shimmer.

The common mistake: Accidentally touching the high e string with the index finger's tip, muting it. The index finger is on B string fret 1 — its tip must curl away from the high e string completely. Check this by playing the chord then plucking the high e string alone. It should ring clean and open.

Practice the Fmaj7 specifically: Strum the chord and then immediately pluck just the high e string. If it rings cleanly, the shape is correct. If it buzzes or sounds muted, adjust your index finger position.

TECH 03 //
PLAYING AT 68 BPM WITHOUT DRAGGING

The paradox of slow tempo: Playing accurately at 68 BPM is harder than it sounds. Most guitarists naturally rush slow songs — the gap between strums is uncomfortable and the instinct is to fill it. If you rush this song by even 5 BPM it loses its atmosphere entirely and starts to feel nervous rather than dreamlike.

The fix: Use a metronome when practicing, but practice with the metronome clicking on beats 2 and 4 only (the "backbeat" setting on most metronomes). This forces you to feel the space between the clicks rather than rushing toward them. The 68 BPM space is where the song lives — get comfortable in it.

The test: Record yourself playing one verse. Play the recording back at 1.5x speed. Does it now sound like a normal-tempo strummed song? If yes, the feel was correct. If it still sounds slow or dragging at 1.5x, you were rushing during the recording.

// What Good Execution Sounds Like
The intro creates immediate space — the Am → Asus4 oscillation floating above the Fmaj7 shimmer creates a cathedral-like openness that no other song in the set replicates. The verse is hypnotic in its repetition — the same chord motion cycling, the Asus4/F# arriving like the bass dropping slightly beneath the melody. The chorus opens completely — G and Fmaj7, two wide chords resonating fully at 68 BPM. The synth solo section breathes without agenda. The outro builds with each repetition until the final Am lands like a period at the end of a long sentence. At no point does the guitar compete with the atmosphere. It creates the atmosphere. In a metal arrangement this song does not need to be heavy — it needs to be wide, resonant, and precisely controlled at exactly 68 BPM. That is harder than heavy.
