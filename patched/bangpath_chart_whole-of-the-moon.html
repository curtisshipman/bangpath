// The Slash Chord Song

The Whole of the Moon is defined by slash chords — five of its nine chords have a different bass note than the chord root. G/C, G/F, G/B, C/E, and Dm7 all create a specific harmonic colour that gives this song its soaring, orchestral feel. The good news: as a rhythm guitarist, slash chords are mostly about what the bass player does. You play the top chord (G, C etc); the bassist provides the bass note. Your G/C is just a G chord. Your G/F is just a G chord with a different bass underneath. Understanding this makes the chord chart much less intimidating.

The piano tab (132 bars, treble and bass clef — not guitar tab) confirms the song structure section by section and aligns with the chord chart exactly. Five verses, a bridge, a trumpet solo section, and an extended outro.

// How Slash Chords Work for Rhythm Guitar

A slash chord like G/C means "G chord with C in the bass." As rhythm guitarist, you play the G chord. The bassist plays C in the low register. Together you create the G/C sound. You don't need to put a C note in your guitar chord unless you specifically want to — the bass handles it. This simplifies everything.

G/C
You play: G chord
Bass plays: C note
Where: Intro, verse, outro — most common
G/F
You play: G chord
Bass plays: F note
Where: Verse and bridge — creates descending bass line
G/B
You play: G chord
Bass plays: B note
Where: Bridge only — part of descending line
C/E
You play: C chord
Bass plays: E note
Where: Bridge only — creates smooth voice leading
Dm7
You play: Dm7 chord (Dm with open B)
Bass plays: D note
Where: Bridge only — adds colour

// The Good News About Slash Chords Here

G/C and G/F are played with the exact same G chord shape on guitar. The difference is only in the bass. This means your hand doesn't move between G/C and G/F — you hold the same G barre chord and the bassist changes the root underneath you. In practice: learn G/C and G/F as one chord shape. C/E is just a C chord with the low E string included (which you'd normally mute). G/B is a partial G chord. All manageable.

// What Makes This Pattern Distinctive

The upstrokes on beats 1& and 3& (not 2& and 4& as in most patterns) give this song a specific forward-rolling feel. Most syncopated patterns accent the backbeat (2 and 4) — this one creates lift on beats 1 and 3 instead. This matches the song's soaring, upward emotional trajectory. Practice the upstroke placement carefully — hitting 2& and 4& instead feels similar but sounds different.

The descending bass line: G/B → Am7 → G creates a chromatic bass descent: B → A → G. The bass player walks down; you hold the G chord shape (with the G/B partial voicing — skip A and E strings), then shift to Am7, then full G. These are quick changes: one chord per half-bar or per beat. Practice the G/B → Am7 → G sequence separately at 70 BPM before integrating into the full bridge.

The outro decision: The original fades out on G/C → C. In your metal arrangement, pick a clean ending point — either a final big C chord with a hard stop, or a final G/C → C with a drum crash cutoff. Whatever you choose, rehearse it as deliberately as every other section. A well-executed ending is as important as a well-executed opening.
06 //
TECHNIQUE NOTES
THREE THINGS TO MASTER
TECH 01 //
PATTERN UPSTROKE PLACEMENT

The specific challenge: Upstrokes on beats 1& and 3& — not the more common 2& and 4&. This feels counterintuitive at first because most strum patterns accent the backbeat. This pattern accents beats 1 and 3 with an upstroke lift, giving the song its forward momentum.

Drill: Count aloud "1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and" while playing. The upstroke happens on "1 and" and "3 and." Beats "2 and" and "4 and" are skips — arm moves but misses strings. Do this with open strings at 70 BPM for two minutes before adding any chords.

TECH 02 //
F BARRE ENDURANCE — AGAIN

Same problem as True Faith, longer song: F at fret 1 appears in every verse, the intro, the trumpet solo, the bridge, and the outro. Over nearly 5 minutes this is a significant barre endurance test. The F barre needs to be completely automatic — no conscious effort, no audible hesitation when it arrives.

Endurance drill: Play C → F → C → F at 90 BPM continuously for 3 minutes. Focus on how the F barre sounds on bar 45 vs bar 5. If it's degrading, your mechanics are off. The barre shouldn't require more effort as the song progresses — it should feel identical throughout.

TECH 03 //
BRIDGE TRANSITION DRILL — G/B · AM7 · G

The bridge is the only genuinely new chord territory: G/B (partial G), Am7 (open), and Dm7 don't appear anywhere else in the song. The G/B → Am7 → G sequence moves quickly and needs its own drill time before the bridge feels comfortable.

Drill: Run G/B → Am7 → G at 60 BPM, one chord per beat. G/B: play top four strings of G chord only (D through e). Am7: open Am7 shape. G: full G chord. The transition from G/B to Am7 is the trickiest — fretting hand has to release the partial G and re-form Am7 quickly. 5 minutes daily until the bridge section feels as natural as the verse.

// What Good Execution Sounds Like
The song opens with an immediate sense of space and lift — the C major key and the G/C voicing create natural resonance on a standard-tuned guitar. The upstroke on beat 1& gives every bar a slight forward lean. The verse cycling between C/G/C and F/G/F feels effortless — no audible chord changes, just the sound transforming between two emotional states. The bridge introduces new colour without disrupting the momentum. The trumpet solo section (handled by your lead guitarist) floats above an invisible, locked rhythm guitar. The outro G/C → C alternation winds down with decreasing tension until the final C chord lands with complete conviction. Five minutes of consistent execution. No dropped barre chords. No hesitations. The pattern sounds like it could run forever.
